HISTORICAL VALUE GUIDE

Retrospective Appraisal Guide

A retrospective appraisal reconstructs both the market and the property's condition as of a prior effective date. It is not today's value discounted backward by a simple percentage.

Updated August 17, 2026. General appraisal information only; not legal, tax, lending, engineering or accounting advice.

Confirm the exact historical date

The date may relate to death, separation, transfer, litigation, bankruptcy or tax planning. Confirm it with the appropriate advisor before engagement.

Changing the date can change comparable selection, trend analysis and property-condition assumptions, so document it in writing.

Reconstruct the property

A current inspection documents today's condition. Historical photos, listings, permits, renovation records, insurance files and prior reports may be needed to understand the earlier condition.

Separate improvements completed before the date from later work. Unverified facts may require disclosed assumptions or limitations.

Research the correct market period

Comparable sales should reflect the market around the historical date, with attention to contract timing, financing and changing local supply and demand.

Current sales may provide context but cannot automatically substitute for evidence that informed buyers and sellers at the time.

Use historical records carefully

Tax records, zoning maps, school boundaries and public descriptions may have changed. Archived sources and their limitations should be retained and explained.

For rural, waterfront and custom homes, archived listings may be especially useful because public records often omit view, access, condition and accessory improvements.

Allow additional research time

Older dates and unusual properties may require archived data and reconciliation of conflicting evidence. Provide available documents early.

The resulting opinion is tied to the stated historical date and use. It is not today's list price, lending value or a forecast.

Planning checklist

  • Confirm historical effective date
  • Identify client and intended users
  • Collect historical photos
  • Provide archived listings
  • Document renovation dates
  • Provide deeds and surveys
  • Separate current from historical condition
  • Allow archived-data research time
  • Identify unresolved facts
  • Use report only as intended

Frequently asked questions

Is a current inspection still useful?

It can identify enduring characteristics, but the appraiser must still reconstruct condition as of the historical date.

How far back can value be developed?

Feasibility depends on reliable historical property and market evidence, not a universal time limit.

Can an index be used alone?

An index may provide context but usually does not replace property-specific analysis.

What if no historical photos exist?

Other records may help, but uncertainty and assumptions must be disclosed.
